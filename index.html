Reading

Six stops, one question.

Junior English follows one big idea all year: every story about power is also a story about which words survive. This line follows that idea from a dragon's price list, to a tyrant's dictionary, to a stolen crown, then turns the same question back on the students in the room. Most days open with a knights-and-knaves puzzle or a thought experiment to sharpen critical thinking, and media criticism runs through the whole year. The last stop is their own writing, judged by the same rules as everyone else's.

01 Rhetoric Not decoration. The basic structure that holds up every argument. In planning

Twelve Angry Men, 1957. One juror votes not guilty against eleven others, then has to build a case for it, one person at a time.

02 1984 Not the cameras. The dictionary. Open · reading room

Apple's famous 1984 ad: advertising, its own kind of propaganda, borrowing Orwell's ideas to sell a computer.

03 Home Fire Whose grief the world takes seriously, and whose gets called a threat instead. In planning

A 2019 film, also called Antigone, that moves the same myth into modern Montreal: a brother's death, a family standing against the state to bury him properly.

04 Beowulf The dragon's the least interesting part. What happens after the killing — who owes what — is the real point. In planning

The British Library reading Beowulf's opening lines aloud, in the actual Old English it was written in.

05 Macbeth A stolen crown, and a price that never stops climbing. In planning

Ian McKellen, 1979, breaking down "tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" line by line: what the words are actually doing, not just what they mean.

06 Personal narrative Every argument up to this point was about somebody else's words. This one's about yours. In planning

The last stop on the line, and the one where somebody else is finally reading you. Same rules as everyone else's.

// Notices Posted for families

For families, plainly

  • Attendance: miss more than nine unexcused days in a full-year class, and you don't get credit for that class. That's district policy, not something made up just for this class.
  • Devices: phones go in the classroom's phone caddy (a small storage rack) during class time. New this year, districtwide: phones stay away from the first bell to the last, all day, not just in this class.
  • Grading: grades are based on percentages. For the first three marking periods, the lowest grade possible on anything is 50%, even if the real score was lower. Grades are about the schoolwork itself, not about behavior in class.
  • Academic honesty: cheating and plagiarism both start with a zero the first time it happens. That includes using AI tools without permission.
  • Homework: there isn't much of it, on purpose. Most of it is grammar practice and reading. When there's a reading check, it's just to see if the reading got done, not a hard test.

The full policy is written out in the student handbook. There's a plainer version of all of this on the syllabus page. Questions → Contact.
